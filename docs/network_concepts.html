0 Warm-up: what you already know

You said in-degree, degree and betweenness feel fine, so here they are in one line each. Keep them in mind, because everything below builds on them.

  • In-degree in a citation network = how many papers in your corpus cite this paper. It is smaller than OpenAlex's cited_by_count, which counts citations from the whole world.
  • Degree centrality in the co-authorship network = how many distinct people someone has written with (networkx divides it by N − 1).
  • Betweenness = how often someone sits on the shortest path between two other people. High betweenness means "bridge". On a graph with thousands of authors, use the sampled version: nx.betweenness_centrality(H, k=500, seed=42).

In-degree has one weakness, and it motivates the first new idea: it treats every citation as equal. A citation from a landmark paper counts the same as one from a paper nobody reads.

1 PageRank

The idea in one sentence

This is how Google ranked web pages in 1998. Swap "web page" for "paper" and "link" for "citation".

A paper is important if important papers cite it. That sounds circular, and it is. PageRank solves the circle by letting importance flow around the network until it settles.

Think of it as a voting game:

  1. Every paper starts with the same amount of "importance", say 1/N each.
  2. In each round, every paper splits its importance equally among the papers it cites. If a paper cites 2 others, each gets half.
  3. Every paper's new importance is the sum of what it received.
  4. Repeat until the numbers stop changing.

Two consequences fall out right away. A citation from a paper that is itself important passes along more. And a citation from a paper that cites 50 things passes along very little, because it is split 50 ways.

A tiny example: four papers

Arrows point from the citing paper to the cited paper. A cites B and C. B cites C. C cites A. D cites C. Before you press anything, guess: which paper will end up most important? Which will be least?

What you should see: C gets the most (three papers cite it). A comes a close second even though only one paper cites it, because that one paper is C, the most important paper, and C gives A all of its importance. D, which nobody cites, ends lowest.

Why damping exists

Switch damping off and watch: the numbers bounce around for many rounds, and D drops to exactly 0.

Without damping, two things go wrong:

  • Papers nobody cites get 0, and importance can slosh back and forth in cycles (A→C→A) instead of settling quickly.
  • Dead ends leak importance. A paper whose references are all outside your corpus (very common: a 2016 paper citing 1990s work) has nobody to pass its score to. Importance flows in and never comes out.

The fix is the random surfer story. Imagine a reader who starts on a random paper and keeps clicking a random reference. 85% of the time they follow a reference. 15% of the time they get bored and jump to a completely random paper. A paper's PageRank is the fraction of time this reader spends on it. The 0.85 is the damping factor d (networkx calls it alpha).

PR(i) = (1 − d)/N + d · Σj cites i PR(j) / out(j)
N
number of papers in the network
d
damping factor, almost always 0.85
out(j)
how many papers j cites (its out-degree), so j's vote is split out(j) ways
(1 − d)/N
the "bored reader jumps anywhere" share that every paper gets for free

Dead-end papers (out-degree 0) are handled by pretending they cite every paper equally. networkx does this for you automatically.

Do one round by hand

Start with everything at 0.25. Paper C is cited by A (which cites 2 papers), B (cites 1) and D (cites 1):

PR(C) = 0.15/4 + 0.85 × ( 0.25/2 + 0.25/1 + 0.25/1 )
      = 0.0375 + 0.85 × 0.625
      = 0.0375 + 0.53125
      = 0.56875        # matches row t = 1 in the table above

That is the whole algorithm. The computer just repeats this for every paper, many times.

Now on a citation network

Here is a made-up corpus of 12 papers, 6 from IIT Kharagpur (K1–K6) and 6 from IIT Roorkee (R1–R6). Time runs left to right, so arrows always point backwards: you can only cite older work.

Flip between the two sizing modes and look at the table. K3 has the most citations (4), yet three papers outrank it on PageRank. K2 is cited only once, but that one citation comes from K3, and K3 cites nothing else, so K3 passes all its importance to K2. K2 then passes it all to K1. That is "important papers cite you" working exactly as designed.

Age bias. Because citations always point to older papers, importance drains backwards in time and pools in the oldest papers (K1 and R1 here). New 2024–25 papers cannot have earned citations yet. In your report, either say this out loud or compare PageRank only within the same publication year.

In code

import networkx as nx
import pandas as pd

cit = pd.read_csv("citation_network.csv")       # source_doi cites target_doi
G = nx.DiGraph()
G.add_edges_from(zip(cit.source_doi, cit.target_doi))

pr  = nx.pagerank(G, alpha=0.85)                 # dict: doi -> score (sums to 1)
ind = dict(G.in_degree())

top10 = sorted(pr, key=pr.get, reverse=True)[:10]
for doi in top10:
    print(doi, round(pr[doi], 4), ind[doi])

Put the top 10 next to their in-degree in the report. The interesting story is where the two lists disagree.

Further reading

PageRank Algorithm: The Mathematics of Google Search (Cornell, Lecture 3). It covers the same ground in matrix form: the random surfer, dangling nodes, disconnected components, the power method and why the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees one answer. One notation difference: it writes damping as m = 0.15, the probability of jumping to a random page. That is the same model as d = 0.85 here, since m = 1 − d.

2 Communities: Louvain and Leiden

What is a community?

In the co-authorship network, a community is a group of people who write with each other a lot and with outsiders much less. Think of a research group or a lab. Nobody tells the algorithm about departments or institutes. It finds groups from the edges alone, and afterwards you check whether those groups match departments, cut across them, or span both IITs.

To find communities, we need a score for how good a split into groups is. Louvain and Leiden are both just clever ways of searching for a split with a high score. The score is called modularity.

Modularity: comparing with a random null model

This is the null-model idea from class: compare what you see to what a random graph would give.

Saying "lots of edges inside groups" is not enough. If you put everyone in one giant group, every edge is inside it. So modularity asks a sharper question: are there more edges inside each group than you would expect if the edges were wired up at random?

Q = Σcommunities c [ Lc/m − ( dc/2m )² ]
m
total number of edges in the graph
Lc
edges with both ends inside community c
dc
sum of the degrees of the nodes in c
Lc/m
fraction of edges that actually fall inside c
(dc/2m)²
fraction you would expect inside c if you rewired edges randomly but kept everyone's degree (the "configuration model", a cousin of the ER model)

Q near 0 means "no better than random". Real networks with clear groups usually score about 0.3–0.7. Q can even go negative if you split badly.

Try it yourself

Eight faculty, 11 co-authorship edges. Click a person to change their community (blue → orange → green). Try to get the highest Q you can.

Check the natural split by hand. The left group {a, b, c, d} has 5 internal edges. Its degrees are a = 2, b = 3, c = 3, d = 3 (d's edge to e still counts towards d's degree), so dc = 11. The right group is a mirror image.

one side:  5/11 − (11/22)²  =  0.4545 − 0.25  =  0.2045
both sides:                    Q  =  2 × 0.2045  =  0.409

"Everyone together" gives exactly 0: all 11 edges are inside, but you would also expect all of them inside, so there is no surprise. Putting each person in a group alone scores −0.128.

Louvain: a greedy search for high Q

With 8 people you can try splits by hand. With 3,000 faculty the number of possible splits is astronomically large. Louvain (named after the Belgian university where it was invented) finds a good one fast by repeating two phases.

  1. Local moving. Start with everyone alone. Visit each node in turn and ask: "if I moved you into one of your neighbours' communities, which move raises Q the most?" Make that move if it helps. Keep sweeping over all nodes until a full sweep changes nothing.
  2. Aggregation. Collapse each community into a single "super-node". Edges inside a community become a self-loop, and edges between communities add up into one weighted edge. Then go back to phase 1 on this smaller graph.

Leiden: Louvain with a safety check

Leiden came out of Leiden University in 2019. Same goal (high Q), better guarantees.

Louvain has a known flaw. Sometimes a node that was holding a community together moves away, and the community is left as two pieces with no edge between them that still carry the same label. That is clearly not a real "group".

Leiden adds a refinement phase between local moving and aggregation. Inside each community, it rebuilds the group from small well-connected pieces, so a disconnected community gets split before it is collapsed. Leiden guarantees every community it returns is connected, and it is usually faster too. Either is fine for A2. Leiden is the safer modern choice; Louvain is built into networkx.

Two practical points

  • Randomness. Both algorithms visit nodes in a random order, so different runs can give slightly different answers. Fix the seed so your results can be reproduced, and if you have time, check that the main groups survive a few different seeds.
  • Resolution. Both take a resolution parameter (default 1). Higher values give more, smaller communities; lower values give fewer, bigger ones.

In code

auth = pd.read_csv("authorship_network.csv")

H = nx.Graph()
for r in auth.itertuples():                      # weight = number of joint papers
    if H.has_edge(r.author_a, r.author_b):
        H[r.author_a][r.author_b]["weight"] += 1
    else:
        H.add_edge(r.author_a, r.author_b, weight=1)

# Louvain (built into networkx)
comms = nx.community.louvain_communities(H, weight="weight", seed=42)
Q = nx.community.modularity(H, comms, weight="weight")
print(len(comms), "communities, Q =", round(Q, 3))

# Leiden (pip install igraph leidenalg)
import igraph as ig, leidenalg as la
g = ig.Graph.from_networkx(H)
part = la.find_partition(g, la.ModularityVertexPartition, weights="weight", seed=42)
print(len(part), "communities, Q =", round(part.modularity, 3))

For the report: number of communities, the sizes of the largest few, Q, and one sentence per big community about who is in it (mostly one department? both IITs?).

3 Flagging cross-institution edges

This one sounds technical but is really just bookkeeping. Every node carries a label: which institute it belongs to. An edge is cross-institution when its two ends have different labels. "Flagging" means adding a TRUE/FALSE column that says so.

From one paper to edges

Say a single paper, DOI 10.1000/xyz123 from 2022, has four authors:

  • P. Sen, IIT Kharagpur
  • R. Ghosh, IIT Kharagpur
  • A. Rawat, IIT Roorkee
  • M. Iyer, IISc Bangalore

Every pair of co-authors gets an edge. With k authors that is k(k−1)/2 pairs, so 4 authors give 6 rows in authorship_network.csv:

The first six columns are exactly the required CSV schema. The two flag columns are derived by comparing inst_a and inst_b:

  • cross_inst: the two institutes differ. Here 5 of 6 edges.
  • cross_cluster: the two institutes are in different clusters from the course PPT. Kharagpur and Roorkee are both "old IITs", so that edge is not cross-cluster. IIT ↔ IISc is. The PPT's examples are IIT ↔ CSIR, IIT ↔ IISER and CSIR ↔ DAE.

You'll need a small lookup table: institute name → cluster. Write it once by hand from the PPT.

The same thing for citations

Go back to the 12-paper citation network and tick Highlight cross-institution citations. Five of the 14 citations go between Kharagpur and Roorkee: three Kharagpur papers citing Roorkee work, and two the other way round. Counting these per direction gives you the in-flow and out-flow numbers for section 7a of the brief.

Three traps in real data

  • Huge author lists. A particle-physics paper with 2,000 authors makes about 2 million edges and swamps everything. Pick a cutoff (for example, skip papers with more than 50 authors for the network), and state it in the report.
  • Multiple affiliations. OpenAlex can list two institutes for one author on one paper. Decide on a rule (for example, prefer your assigned institute) and apply it the same way everywhere.
  • Name variants. "P. Sen", "Prasanta Sen" and "P. K. Sen" may be one person. Use OpenAlex author IDs as node IDs and keep names only as labels.
cluster = {"IIT Kharagpur": "Old IIT", "IIT Roorkee": "Old IIT",
           "IISc Bangalore": "IISc/TIFR/IISER", "CSIR-NCL Pune": "CSIR Chem", ...}

auth["cross_inst"]    = auth.inst_a != auth.inst_b
auth["cross_cluster"] = auth.inst_a.map(cluster) != auth.inst_b.map(cluster)
cit["cross_inst"]     = cit.source_inst != cit.target_inst

4 Jaccard similarity

Jaccard similarity measures how much two sets overlap, on a scale from 0 (nothing in common) to 1 (identical).

J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = (things in both) / (things in either)

The smallest example

Your fruit basket is {apple, banana, mango}. Mine is {banana, mango, grape, kiwi}. We share 2 fruits (banana, mango), and between us there are 5 different fruits. So J = 2/5 = 0.4.

Why divide by the union and not just count the overlap? So a huge set doesn't look similar to everything just because it's huge.

Why not just count the shared items? Because a giant institute that works on 200 topics would "share" many topics with everyone purely by being big. Dividing by the union corrects for size.

For A2: research topics of two institutes

Each institute gets a set of research topics, for example the top 10 OpenAlex subfields by number of papers. Then J tells you how similar two institutes' research profiles are. Toggle topics on and off below:

The partner ranking you would put in the report

Combine Jaccard with the co-authorship counts and you can ask an interesting question: do we collaborate most with institutes that do similar research, or with ones that complement us?

In this invented data, IISc shares the most papers, but Roorkee has the most similar research profile (J = 0.60). CSIR-NCL overlaps least (J = 0.20), so its 74 joint papers are probably a complementary collaboration: chemistry expertise that Kharagpur doesn't have in-house. One observation like that makes a good paragraph in the report.

Bonus: Jaccard on a network

The same formula works on neighbour sets. Two faculty with J = 0.8 between their co-author lists move in the same circle even if they have never written together, which makes them a good guess for a future collaboration. networkx has it built in: nx.jaccard_coefficient(H, [(u, v)]).

def jaccard(A, B):
    A, B = set(A), set(B)
    return len(A & B) / len(A | B) if (A | B) else 0.0

kgp_topics  = set(top_subfields(df[df.institution == "IIT Kharagpur"], n=10))
iisc_topics = set(top_subfields(partner_papers["IISc Bangalore"], n=10))
print(jaccard(kgp_topics, iisc_topics))

top_subfields is yours to write: group by OpenAlex topic/subfield, count, take the top n.
