\begin{abstract}
We collected the science and engineering papers of IIT Kharagpur and IIT Roorkee from 2016 to 2026 and linked them to
1,410 faculty members. We study them at the institution, department and faculty level, and we build a citation network
and a co-authorship network. Both IITs almost doubled their output, and most of their journal papers are in Q1 journals.
Their faculty h-index is about half of a career h-index, because we count only ten years of papers. Citations are
heavy-tailed and grow by preferential attachment, but 88\% of the citations inside an institute are self-citations, so
we also rank papers without them. Faculty work in tight, hierarchical groups inside their own IIT. The collaboration
network is a small world that grew denser every year, and it splits into its two campuses when about 100 bridging
faculty are removed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%===========================================================
We look at research at three levels, the \textbf{institution}, its \textbf{departments} and its \textbf{faculty}.
Counts of papers and citations describe each level, but they do not show how the research is \emph{connected}. Networks
show this. A \textbf{citation network} shows how ideas move between papers. A \textbf{co-authorship network} shows how
people work together.

This report follows that order. It goes from the institution to the faculty, then to the two networks, and then to the
links with other Indian institutes. Table~\ref{tab:glance} gives the main numbers.

\section{Data and Method}\label{sec:data}
%===========================================================
\textbf{Collecting the papers.} We scraped publication records from public sources. These are Crossref, Europe PMC,
DOAJ, INSPIRE, ORCID, DBLP and Semantic Scholar. We searched in two ways. The first way uses the institute name in the
author affiliations. The second way uses the faculty list of each IIT. This gave 33,393 records for KGP and 25,775
records for Roorkee.

\textbf{Filling in missing values.} We used \textbf{OpenAlex} to get the DOI, type, venue, ISSN, citations,
references, author affiliations and research topic. We used \textbf{Scimago} to get the journal quartile, by ISSN and
publication year, and \textbf{CORE} to get the conference rank. Later we also took from OpenAlex the yearly citation
counts of each paper, the impact factor of each journal and the career profile of each faculty member. We did not guess
any value. If we could not find a value, we left it blank.

\textbf{Checking the affiliation.} We kept a paper only if the IIT is in the author affiliations on the paper. This
removes namesakes and papers from before a person joined the IIT.

\textbf{Scope.} We keep only \textbf{science and engineering}, as the professor asked. A paper is kept if its OpenAlex
topic is in the Physical Sciences or Life Sciences. Health Sciences and Social Sciences papers are left out. We also
leave out the faculty of humanities, management, law, education and medical units (for example HSS, the Vinod Gupta
School of Management, the School of Medical Science and Technology and the B C Roy hospital). Their science papers stay,
and they count for the institute. This scope removed 4,069 papers at KGP and 2,518 at Roorkee. We are left with
\textbf{25,950} papers for KGP and \textbf{20,326} for Roorkee.

\textbf{Linking papers to faculty.} The brief asks for one row for each paper and faculty member. We linked papers to
faculty with five rules, from the most reliable to the least reliable. These are the \textbf{ORCID}, the faculty
profile where we found the paper, the exact \textbf{name}, the \textbf{name and department}, and the OpenAlex author
ID. Extra checks stop wrong matches, for example two faculty with the same common surname. 82.8\% of KGP papers and
84.4\% of Roorkee papers have at least one faculty member. We credited the other papers to the institute.

\textbf{Deliverables.} The four files have 54,826 rows (\texttt{group3\_data}), 489,869 citation edges, 137,473
author pairs and 5,367 Indian partners. \texttt{group3\_data} also has the number of authors of each paper and a flag
for papers with more than 10 authors.

\textbf{Limits.} 2026 is a \textbf{partial year}, and the citation counts are from October 2026. The h-index and
i10-index use only the 2016--2026 papers in our dataset (Section~\ref{sec:hlow} shows what this means).

\section{Institution-Level Statistics}\label{sec:inst}

\subsection{Publication trend}
The simplest measure of research activity is the \textbf{number of papers per year}. We look at it first because it
sets the scale for all other results. More papers give more citations and larger networks. Thus we must keep the size
of each IIT in mind when we compare them.

Both IITs grew steadily (Fig.~\ref{fig:trend}). KGP went from 1,583 papers in 2016 to 2,914 in 2025, which is
\textbf{1.8 times} more. Its peak was 3,094 papers in 2024. Roorkee went from 1,186 papers to 2,575, which is
\textbf{2.2 times} more. KGP is larger every year, but Roorkee grows faster. Not all paper types grew at the same rate.
At KGP, conference papers grew 2.4 times and journal papers grew 1.6 times. At Roorkee the values are 2.9 and 1.9.
Preprints went from almost zero to 130 to 200 each year. Thus part of the growth is a change in \emph{how} the IITs
publish, and not only in how much. The low 2026 bars are because the year is not complete.

\subsection{Journal quality}
A paper count does not show quality. The \textbf{Scimago quartile} ranks each journal in its subject by its SJR score.
Then it divides the list into four equal parts. Q1 is the top 25\%. Scimago ranks journals \emph{inside} each subject.
Thus the Q1 share lets us compare quality across fields that cite at very different rates.

About 95\% of the journal papers of both IITs have a quartile. Of these papers, \textbf{66.0\%} at KGP and
\textbf{60.7\%} at Roorkee are in Q1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:quart}). Between 27\% and 30\% are in Q2, and only about 1\% are in
Q4. We expect this result from two of the oldest IITs, which publish mostly in established journals.

\subsection{Impact factor}
The quartile puts a journal in one of four bands. The \textbf{impact factor} (IF) gives a number. It is the number of
citations a journal received in one year to its papers of the two years before, divided by the number of those papers.
The official Journal Impact Factor of Clarivate is not free. Thus we use the same formula on OpenAlex data, which
OpenAlex calls the \emph{2-year mean citedness}. Two things are different from the official IF. It is the current
value, not the value in the year of publication. It is also usually higher, because OpenAlex counts more citing
sources. For example, Scientific Reports has 5.2 here and about 3.8 in Clarivate. Thus the thresholds below are on the
OpenAlex scale.

The median journal paper is in a journal with IF 4.1 at KGP and 4.0 at Roorkee. \textbf{36.0\%} of KGP journal papers
and \textbf{34.1\%} of Roorkee journal papers are in journals with IF $>$ 5. \textbf{6.1\%} and \textbf{5.3\%} are in
journals with IF $>$ 10 (1,148 and 797 papers). The share above 5 grew a little over the decade, from about 30\% in 2017--2019 to between
36\% and 39\% since 2021 (Fig.~\ref{fig:if}). This agrees with the Q1 share above, and KGP is a little ahead on both.

\subsection{Conferences and CORE}
For conference papers we use the \textbf{CORE} rank (A*, A, B, C). CORE ranks mostly computing conferences. Thus it
tells us about the computer science side of each IIT, and most conference papers have no CORE rank.

At KGP, 522 of 3,814 conference papers (13.7\%) are in CORE-ranked venues. These are 98 A*, 80 A, 222 B and 122 C
papers. At Roorkee, 289 of 3,211 (9.0\%) are ranked. These are 11 A*, 34 A, 144 B and 100 C papers. Thus KGP is far
ahead at the top ranks. This result makes sense. Computer Science is 14.1\% of KGP papers and only 8.9\% of Roorkee
papers. The most frequent CORE venue is the same for both IITs. It is the IEEE Geoscience and Remote Sensing
Symposium (rank C, 51 KGP papers and 81 Roorkee papers). The KGP list also has top venues such as AAAI (A*, 16
papers) and CIKM (A, 17 papers). Ranked conference papers increased after 2021 at both IITs.

\subsection{Research areas and their categories}
Next we ask \emph{what} each IIT works on. OpenAlex puts every paper in a hierarchy of four levels. These are the
\emph{domain}, the \emph{field}, the \emph{subfield} and the \emph{topic}. For example, a paper on perovskite solar
cells is in the domain Physical Sciences, the field Engineering, the subfield Electrical and Electronic Engineering and
the topic Perovskite Materials and Applications. The research areas are important for the rest of the report. They
control which papers can cite each other and which faculty can work together.

\textbf{Engineering} is the largest field at both IITs, with 36.6\% of KGP papers and 43.3\% of Roorkee papers
(Fig.~\ref{fig:areas}). Inside Engineering the main subfields are Electrical and Electronic Engineering (24\% at KGP
and 32\% at Roorkee), Mechanical Engineering, Biomedical Engineering and Civil and Structural Engineering. After
Engineering, KGP has more Computer Science (14.1\%) and Roorkee has more Environmental Science (12.7\%). Materials
Science is about 11\% at both. The dashboard lists the subfields inside every field.

\subsection{How much do large collaborations matter?}
To see how much our numbers depend on these papers, we computed every headline number again without them
(Fig.~\ref{fig:large}, left). The \textbf{typical} paper does not change. The median paper still has 6 citations at
KGP and 5 at Roorkee, and the Q1 share falls by only half a point. The \textbf{top} changes. At KGP, \textbf{6 of the
10 most cited papers} have more than 10 authors (2 at Roorkee). Without them, KGP loses 9.3\% of its papers with 100
or more citations, and its institute h-index falls from 184 to 176. Roorkee's falls from 156 to 154. The faculty
h-index also moves. 74 KGP faculty (9.6\%) and 57 Roorkee faculty (8.9\%) lose at least one point, and the median
KGP faculty h-index falls from 8 to 7.

\textbf{A citation premium.} Large papers are cited more than other papers of the same IIT and the same year
(Fig.~\ref{fig:large}, right). At KGP the median large paper gets \textbf{2.7 times} the citations of the median other
paper (median over 2016--2024). At Roorkee the premium is smaller, 1.25 times. One reason is that more authors means
more people who cite the paper and more networks that see it.

\textbf{What these papers are.} 82\% of the large journal papers at KGP are in Q1 journals, against 62\% for its other
papers (Roorkee 76\% against 57\%). Only 2--3\% are conference papers, against 15--16\% of the other papers. And
\textbf{81\%} of them have a co-author abroad (Roorkee 76\%), against 22--23\% of the other papers. Thus large teams
mostly mean international collaboration. Most of them are not huge. 86\% of the KGP and 76\% of the Roorkee large
papers have 11 to 20 authors. Only 17 and 7 papers have 100 or more.

\textbf{Where they come from.} At KGP they come from materials research: Metallurgical and Materials Engineering
(78 papers), Physics (60), Agricultural and Food Engineering (60) and Biosciences (45). Materials Science is the largest
field (22\% of them), and the top venues are \emph{Small}, \emph{Advanced Functional Materials} and \emph{ACS Applied
Materials \& Interfaces}. At Roorkee they come mostly from \textbf{Physics} (134 papers, 30\% of Roorkee's large papers
and 9\% of its physics papers), then Biosciences (74) and Hydrology (28). The top venue is \emph{Physical Review C}
(44 papers), from nuclear physics collaborations. The pulsar timing papers are here too.

\textbf{Who depends on them.} For a few faculty the large papers are a large part of the h-index. At KGP the h-index of
Tapas Kumar Maiti (Biosciences) falls from 36 to 30 without them, and that of Abhijit Mukherjee (Geology and
Geophysics) from 44 to 39. At Roorkee the largest drop is in Physics. Bhal Chandra Joshi, who works on pulsar timing
arrays, goes from 8 to 2. This does not make these papers less valuable. But an h-index from large collaborations
measures a different thing than one from a small group. So we show both on the dashboard.

\section{Faculty and Department Level}\label{sec:fac}

The institution numbers are sums over people. Now we look at the people, and then at the departments that group them.

\subsection{h-index statistics}
A faculty member has an \textbf{h-index} of $h$ if $h$ of their papers have at least $h$ citations each. This measure
combines \emph{how much} a person publishes and \emph{how much} other people cite it. One very large paper cannot
increase it alone.

The h-index is strongly right-skewed (Fig.~\ref{fig:h}). Many faculty have a small h-index, and a few have a very large
one. Thus the mean alone is misleading, and we give several statistics (Table~\ref{tab:hstats}). At KGP the mean is
9.6 with a standard deviation of 8.6, and the median is 8. At Roorkee the mean is 8.7 with a standard deviation of 7.6,
and the median is 7. The SD is almost as large as the mean, which again shows the long tail. The highest values are 63
at KGP (Narayan Chandra Das, Rubber Technology) and 53 (Makarand M.\ Ghangrekar, Civil Engineering), and 42 at
Roorkee (Soumitra Satapathi, Physics).

\subsection{i10-index}
The \textbf{i10-index} is the number of papers with at least 10 citations. It is used by Google Scholar. It is simpler
than the h-index, and it shows how many of a person's papers are cited well. The median faculty member has 6 such
papers at KGP and 5 at Roorkee in these ten years. The mean is much higher (13.9 and 11.6), because a few very active
labs have more than 100 such papers each (Table~\ref{tab:hstats}).

\subsection{Why is our h-index low?}\label{sec:hlow}
A median h-index of 7 or 8 looks low for faculty of old IITs. We checked why. For each faculty member we compare three
values. The first is our h-index. The second is the h-index of the same papers with OpenAlex citation counts in place
of ours. The third is the h-index of the person's whole OpenAlex profile, which covers the whole career. We use the
career value only when the profile clearly belongs to the person (at least 5 of their papers checked, and at least
80\% of them have that profile). This leaves 565 faculty at KGP and 437 at Roorkee. Some profiles merge two people with
the same name, and this rule removes them.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Median faculty h-index, three ways}
\label{tab:hcheck}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}

\midrule

Table~\ref{tab:hcheck} gives the answer. The citation source changes very little, because the same papers give almost
the same h-index with OpenAlex counts. The big difference is the \textbf{time window}. Our h-index counts only papers
from 2016 to 2026. A career h-index also counts all the older papers, which have had many years to collect citations.
Thus our value is about \textbf{half} of the career value (53\% at KGP and 50\% at Roorkee at the median), and 98\% of
faculty are below their career value (Fig.~\ref{fig:hcheck}). Google Scholar is higher again, because it also counts
preprints, theses and books. For example, A.\ Taraphder's 2017 PRL paper has 351 citations in our data and 435 in
Google Scholar. The paper is in our dataset and linked to him by ORCID.

\subsection{h-index over the years}
The h-index grows with time, because a person writes more papers and the old papers collect citations. To see this
growth, we compute the h-index at the end of each year. For year $Y$ we use the papers published up to $Y$ and only the
citations received up to $Y$, from the yearly citation counts in OpenAlex.

The mean faculty h-index rose from 0.9 at the end of 2016 to 9.5 at the end of 2025 at KGP, and from 0.8 to 8.5 at
Roorkee (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyear}). It grows by about one point each year at both IITs, and KGP stays a little ahead. The
h-index of each institute as a whole, computed over all its papers, grew from 10 to 178 at KGP and from 12 to 153 at
Roorkee. The growth is steady. This agrees with the growing output and the growing citations
from Indian institutes (Section~\ref{sec:cit}).

\subsection{Departments}
Departments are between the person and the institute. They are the natural unit to compare research areas inside one
IIT. Table~\ref{tab:dept} shows the five departments with the most papers at each IIT.

At KGP, Computer Science has the most papers, but Civil Engineering has the most citations (41,228). Civil Engineering
includes environmental engineering, and two of the five highest h-index values at KGP are in this department. At
Roorkee, Civil Engineering has the most papers and the most faculty (75). The Q1 share is between 50\% and 73\% in the
engineering departments. Mathematics is much lower at both IITs, at about 45\%. We expect this, because mathematics
journals have lower impact scores in general. The mean h-index of a department is close to its SD, so every department
has a mix of young and very senior faculty.

\section{Citation Network}\label{sec:cit}
%===========================================================
Up to now, all citations had the same value. A network lets us ask \emph{who} cites whom.

\subsection{Construction}
Each \textbf{node} is a paper of our two IITs. A \textbf{directed edge} $A \to B$ means that paper $A$ cites paper $B$,
and both papers are in our dataset. The edges have a direction because a citation goes one way, from a newer paper to
an older paper. The network has \textbf{46,072} papers and \textbf{86,090} edges. KGP has 25,942 papers and 47,586
edges, and Roorkee has 20,326 papers and 35,246 edges. The network is very sparse. The density is $4.1\times10^{-5}$
and the mean in-degree is 1.9. About \textbf{half} of the papers get no citation from another paper of the two IITs.
The largest weakly connected component has 53\% of the papers. We expect this, because the papers are in many fields
that do not cite each other.

\subsection{In-degree distribution}
As we know from our lectures already, the in-degree of a node is the number of edges that point to it. Here it is the
number of papers in our dataset that cite a paper. Its distribution shows if attention is spread evenly or goes to a
small number of papers.

Fig.~\ref{fig:indeg} shows $P(\text{in-degree} \ge k)$ on a log-log scale. The curve decreases slowly over two orders
of magnitude. Thus the distribution is \textbf{heavy-tailed}. Most papers get zero, one or two internal citations, but
a few papers get 40 to 58. The curves of the two IITs are almost the same. This tells us that the shape comes from how
citation works, and not from one institute.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{indegree_ccdf.png}
\caption{In-degree distribution of the citation network (CCDF, log-log).}
\label{fig:indeg}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Is it a power law?}
A straight line on a log-log plot is not enough to call a distribution a power law. We follow the method of Clauset,
Shalizi and Newman. It fits $P(k) \propto k^{-\alpha}$ above a cut-off $x_{\min}$ by maximum likelihood. Then it
compares the power law with other heavy-tailed distributions by a likelihood ratio test (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit}).

For the internal in-degree, the fit gives $\alpha = 2.81$ above $x_{\min}=5$. But the \textbf{lognormal} fits much
better (normalised ratio $R=-13.3$, $p<0.001$), because the curve bends down at the end. This is natural. A paper can
get at most a few dozen citations from inside two institutes. For the \textbf{global} citation counts, the tail above
73 citations (1,861 papers) has $\alpha = 3.00 \pm 0.05$. The power law is much better than an exponential ($R=5.4$,
$p<0.001$), but we cannot tell it apart from a lognormal ($p=0.16$). Thus we can say ``heavy-tailed'', but not
``scale-free''. This is also what large studies of citations find.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{citation_distribution_fit.png}
\caption{Maximum-likelihood fits of the tail: power law (grey) and lognormal (orange).}
\label{fig:fit}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Preferential attachment, measured}
In the lectures, the Barab\'asi--Albert model explains heavy tails with \textbf{preferential attachment}: a node with
degree $k$ gets new links at a rate $\Pi(k) \propto k$. With dated citations we can measure this directly. For each
year $T$ from 2017 to 2025, we take every paper that already existed, count its citations $k$ up to year $T-1$, and
count its new citations in year $T$. The mean number of new citations at each $k$ is the attachment rate $A(k)$. We
use the internal citations and the citations from other Indian institutes, because both have a year.

Fig.~\ref{fig:pa} shows that $A(k)$ grows steadily with $k$. A paper with 10 citations gets about three times as many
new citations as a paper with one. The best fit is $A(k) = c\,(k + k_0)^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 0.80 \pm 0.02$ and
$k_0 = 1.8$. Thus the attachment is \textbf{slightly sublinear}. The $k_0$ is the ``initial attractiveness'': an
uncited paper still gets 0.57 new citations a year. A sublinear $\alpha$ gives a stretched-exponential or lognormal
shape rather than a pure power law. This agrees with the fits above. One caution: papers also differ in quality (their
``fitness''), and better papers are both cited earlier and cited later. Part of the measured effect can come from
this.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{preferential_attachment.png}
\caption{Attachment rate $A(k)$: new citations in a year for a paper with $k$ earlier citations.}
\label{fig:pa}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Self-citations}
The brief defines the in-degree as the number of citations from the same institute's papers. We checked who these
citations come from. An edge is a \textbf{self-citation} if the citing and the cited paper share an author (the same
OpenAlex author, or the same faculty member). \textbf{88.5\%} of the 86,090 internal edges are self-citations (KGP
92.8\%, Roorkee 90.1\%). Only 9,909 edges connect different authors. The share falls from 98\% in 2016 to 87--89\% in
2026 (Fig.~\ref{fig:self}). Over time, more colleagues have papers that one can cite.

This changes how we must read the internal network. It mostly shows how researchers build on \emph{their own} earlier
work. 42 papers have zero citations in the world but five or more internal citations, and all of these are
self-citations. 79\% of the edges stay inside one OpenAlex field (field assortativity 0.73), and 76\% of the
non-self edges do too. Thus ideas move between fields only rarely, even between colleagues.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{self_citation_yearly.png}
\caption{Share of internal citations that are self-citations, by year of the citing paper.}
\label{fig:self}
\end{figure}

\subsection{PageRank}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{PageRank} gives a score to a node. The score depends on how many nodes
point to it \emph{and} on how important those nodes are.
\[
PR(i) = \frac{1-\alpha}{N} + \alpha \sum_{j \to i} \frac{PR(j)}{k_{\text{out}}(j)}, \qquad \alpha = 0.85
\]
Here $N$ is the number of papers and $k_{\text{out}}(j)$ is the number of papers that $j$ cites. The in-degree gives
the same value to every citation. PageRank gives more value to a citation from an influential paper. Thus it finds
papers that are the \emph{base} of other well-cited work.

Our data shows this clearly (Table~\ref{tab:pr}). The top paper by PageRank is a 2016 KGP paper on MoS$_2$
heterojunctions. It has only 20 internal citations and its in-degree rank is 195. But the papers that cite it have many
citations themselves. There is also an \textbf{age bias}. Seven of the top ten papers are from 2016, and the Spearman
correlation between PageRank and publication year is $-0.39$. This makes sense. In a citation network the score moves
almost only from newer papers to older papers. Thus the oldest papers in our window collect the most score.

\subsection{Correcting PageRank}
The two problems above, self-citations and age, also affect PageRank. We made two corrections.

First, we ran PageRank on the 9,909 \textbf{non-self} edges. Only 7 of the top 100 papers stay in the top 100. The new
top papers are cited by other groups. Examples are a review of Himalayan--Karakoram glaciers (Roorkee, 331 citations
in the world) and studies of rainfall and drought over India (KGP). Second, we removed the \textbf{age bias} with the
rescaled PageRank of Mariani, Medo and Zhang. Each paper's score becomes a $z$-score among the papers of the same
year: $R_i = (p_i - \mu_{y(i)})/\sigma_{y(i)}$. The test of a fair ranking is simple. Every year should have about 1\%
of its papers in the overall top 1\%. Fig.~\ref{fig:agebias} shows the result. With plain PageRank, 6.5\% of the 2016
papers are in the top 1\%, but no paper from 2024 or 2025. The coefficient of variation of these shares falls from
\textbf{1.44} (PageRank) to 0.87 (no self-citations) and to \textbf{0.20} (rescaled). In the rescaled top 10, papers
from 2023--2025 appear next to older ones. These are the papers that started strongly for their age.

\subsection{Citation flow with other Indian institutes}
The network above stays inside our two IITs. To see their place in Indian research, we also count citations to and
from other Indian institutes. The \textbf{in-flow} is the number of citations from papers of other Indian institutes to
our papers. The \textbf{out-flow} is the number of citations from our papers to their papers. The \textbf{impact
ratio} is the in-flow divided by the out-flow. A ratio above 1 means that Indian research cites the IIT more than the
IIT cites Indian research.

KGP has an in-flow of 102,511 and an out-flow of 115,479, so its ratio is \textbf{0.89}. Roorkee has 86,983 and 107,630,
so its ratio is \textbf{0.81}. At first, it seems that both IITs give more citations than they get. Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}
shows the reason. In 2016 our papers already cited thousands of older Indian papers (the KGP out-flow was 5,335). But
almost no paper had cited them yet (the KGP in-flow was 214), because citations take years to arrive. The in-flow grows
much faster over time. It is larger than the out-flow since \textbf{2023} at KGP and since \textbf{2024} at Roorkee.
Thus the total ratio below 1 comes mostly from the 2016--2026 window.

The largest Indian source of citations to KGP is IIT Roorkee (2,914 citations), followed by VIT and AcSIR. For Roorkee
the largest sources are UPES (2,345), VIT and AcSIR. Large universities such as VIT, UPES and Saveetha cite our IITs
much more than our IITs cite them (ratios between 1.6 and 3.7). The most cited Indian paper that cites KGP is the SciPy
1.0 paper, and the most cited one that cites Roorkee is a paper on drug delivery vehicles for cancer treatment.

\section{Co-authorship Network}\label{sec:auth}
%===========================================================
The citation network shows how \emph{ideas} move. The co-authorship network shows how \emph{people} work together.
This is closer to how research groups really work.

\subsection{Construction}
Each \textbf{node} is a faculty member of one of the two IITs. An \textbf{undirected edge} connects two faculty who
wrote at least one paper together. The edge weight is the number of joint papers. The network has \textbf{1,410}
faculty and \textbf{2,719} edges, and its density is 0.0027. On average, a faculty member has 3.9 faculty co-authors,
and 254 faculty have no faculty co-author. The largest component has 79\% of all faculty. Fig.~\ref{fig:net} shows it.
The two IITs make \textbf{two separate clusters}, and only \textbf{77} edges connect them. This is natural, because the
two campuses are far apart and most work happens inside one campus.

The faculty network is only a small part of all co-authorship. A faculty member has a median of 25 distinct
co-authors. Of all the author pairs in \texttt{authorship\_network.csv} with a faculty member, 52\% are with
students and staff of the same IIT, 21\% with other Indian institutes, 17\% with foreign institutes, and only
\textbf{7\%} with another faculty member of our two IITs. Thus the faculty network shows the links \emph{between}
research groups, and the students fill the groups.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{faculty_network.png}
\caption{Largest component of the faculty network. KGP is blue and Roorkee is orange.}
\label{fig:net}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Degree centrality}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{degree centrality} is the number of neighbours of a node, divided by
$n-1$. In this network it is the number of faculty colleagues that a person wrote papers with. Thus it finds the people
who collaborate the most.

The highest values are for Partha Roy (Roorkee, Biosciences and Bioengineering, 41 faculty co-authors), Suman
Chakraborty (KGP, Mechanical Engineering, 40) and Chandra Sekhar Tiwary (KGP, Metallurgical and Materials Engineering,
36). Work in these fields often needs equipment and skills from several departments, so we expect a high degree
here.

\subsection{Betweenness centrality}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{betweenness centrality} measures how often a node is on the shortest path
between two other nodes. A person with a high betweenness is a \textbf{bridge}. Without this person, some groups are
far apart or not connected. This role is different from having many co-authors.

The top bridges are Partha Roy (0.054) and Pradip K.\ Maji (0.038) at Roorkee, and Ramkrishna Sen (0.035) and Narayan
Chandra Das (0.031) at KGP. Pradip K.\ Maji shows the difference between the two measures. He has only 20 faculty
co-authors, half as many as the top people by degree, but his co-authors are in different groups. Polymer and process
engineering is used by chemists, materials scientists and engineers, so it makes sense that he connects them.

\textbf{Do central faculty have more impact?} Degree and h-index are strongly correlated (Spearman $\rho = 0.70$;
betweenness 0.67). But central faculty also write more papers, and more papers give a higher h-index. If we hold the
number of papers fixed (partial rank correlation), $\rho$ falls to \textbf{0.15} for degree and 0.08 for betweenness.
Thus most of the link is productivity. Working with many colleagues helps the h-index only a little beyond the extra
papers.

\subsection{Communities}
A \textbf{community} is a group of nodes with many edges inside the group and few edges to other groups. We use the
\textbf{Louvain} algorithm, which finds the groups with the highest \textbf{modularity}.
\[
Q = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i,j}\left(A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\right)\delta(c_i, c_j)
\]
The modularity compares the number of edges inside the groups with the number we expect at random. Here the
communities are the real research groups. These groups can follow department lines, or not.

We get $Q = 0.79$ and \textbf{26 communities} with 5 or more faculty. In 23 of these 26 communities, 90\% or more of
the members are from one IIT. This again shows that collaboration stays inside each campus. The communities are
\textbf{interdisciplinary}. In a typical community, the largest department has only about 46\% of the members. For
example, the largest community has 139 faculty, almost all from Roorkee. Biosciences is its largest department, but its
members come from 20 departments. The result is stable. Over 10 random seeds, the mean Adjusted Rand Index is 0.84.
Fig.~\ref{fig:comm} shows the eight largest communities.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{faculty_communities.png}
\caption{Collaboration clusters: the eight largest Louvain communities in the largest component.}
\label{fig:comm}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Communities and departments.} How much do the communities follow the departments? The normalised mutual
information between the two labels is \textbf{0.57}, half-way between ``unrelated'' (0) and ``the same'' (1). 49\% of
the edges join two faculty of the same department, and the department assortativity is 0.47. For the institute it is
0.94. Thus the campus decides almost everything, and the department about half.

\textbf{Louvain or Leiden?} The brief allows both. Louvain can return a community that is not connected inside, and
Leiden fixes this. We ran Leiden (seed 42) and compared. At resolution 1, Leiden finds 25 communities with 5 or more
faculty and $Q = 0.786$, against 26 and $Q = 0.785$ for Louvain, and the two partitions agree well (ARI 0.75). No
community of either method is disconnected. With a resolution sweep (0.25 to 4), the number of communities with 5 or
more faculty goes from 8 to 64, and the large communities split into smaller groups. The structure is
\textbf{hierarchical}: there are groups inside groups.

\subsection{Comparison with random networks}
As we know from our lectures already, the \textbf{clustering coefficient} is the probability that two co-authors of a
person also wrote a paper together. A value such as 0.17 has a meaning only when we compare it with a baseline. Thus we
compare the network with two random models of the same size, with 10 samples each (Table~\ref{tab:null}). The first
model is an \textbf{Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi} (ER) graph with the same number of edges. The second model is a
\textbf{degree-preserving} rewiring that keeps the degree of every node.

The real network has about \textbf{70 times} more clustering than the ER graph and 23 times more than the rewired
graph. But its mean shortest path is almost the same as in the ER graph. This is the \textbf{small-world} property.
The small-world coefficient $\sigma = (C/C_{\text{ER}})/(L/L_{\text{ER}})$ is \textbf{72}, far above 1. The
network has tight local groups, but the paths between people are still short. This makes sense for research groups. A
new project often brings in a colleague of a colleague. The modularity is also much higher than in the two random
models. Thus the communities above are real, and the algorithm did not create them. The positive assortativity (0.12)
shows that well-connected faculty usually work with other well-connected faculty.

\subsection{Core, hierarchy and rich club}
The \textbf{k-core} is the largest group in which every member has at least $k$ co-authors inside the group. The
deepest core is $k=6$ and has 23 faculty, all at KGP, mostly in materials science, metallurgy, rubber technology,
physics and biosciences. This is the dense centre of Fig.~\ref{fig:comm}. The clustering coefficient falls with the
degree as $C(k) \sim k^{-0.50}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rich}, left). Faculty with few co-authors sit in one tight group, and
hubs join several groups that are not linked to each other. This is the signature of a hierarchical network from our
lectures. The \textbf{rich-club coefficient} $\phi(k)$ is the density of links among faculty with more than $k$
co-authors. Divided by its value in the rewired graphs, it rises from 1 to about \textbf{2} for $k \ge 18$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rich}, right). Thus the best-connected faculty work with each other about twice as often as their
degrees alone explain.

\section{Dynamics of the Collaboration Network}\label{sec:dyn}
%===========================================================
Our course is about \emph{dynamical} processes. We look at three of them: how the network grew, how it breaks when
people leave, and how something spreads on it.

\subsection{Growth}
We date each edge by the first paper of the pair and build the network at the end of each year
(Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}). In 2016 there were 312 edges among 361 faculty, and the largest component had only 11\% of
them. In 2017 it had 44\%, and in 2018 already 74\%. This is the emergence of a \textbf{giant component} from the
lectures. It happened while the mean degree grew only from 1.7 to 2.4. Since then the largest component grew to 96\%, and the
mean degree grew from 1.7 to 4.7. The edges grow faster than the nodes, $E \sim N^{1.90}$. This is
\textbf{densification}, as Leskovec and others found for many real networks. The links between the two IITs grew from
4 to 77.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{network_growth.png}
\caption{Growth of the faculty network: largest component, mean degree, and densification ($E$ against $N$, log-log).}
\label{fig:growth}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Robustness}
What happens when faculty leave or retire? We remove faculty step by step and measure the largest component
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). For \textbf{random} removal, the network is robust. We must remove 41\% of the faculty before
the largest component halves. The mean-field theory from the lectures (Molloy--Reed, $\kappa = \langle k^2\rangle /
\langle k\rangle = 9.1$) predicts a breakdown only at $f_c = 1 - 1/(\kappa-1) = 0.88$. For a \textbf{targeted attack}
the network is fragile. Removing the faculty with the highest degree halves it after 14\%. Removing the faculty with
the highest betweenness halves it after only \textbf{7\%}. At that point (102 faculty) the network falls into a
Kharagpur part and a Roorkee part. This is the known weakness of heterogeneous networks: they survive random failure
but not the loss of their hubs and bridges.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{robustness.png}
\caption{Largest component as faculty are removed: random, by degree, by betweenness (both recomputed after each step).}
\label{fig:robust}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Spreading}
Finally, imagine that a new idea or method spreads along the co-author links, like an \textbf{SIR} epidemic. Each
``infected'' faculty member passes it to each co-author with probability $T$, and then stops. This model is exactly
\textbf{bond percolation} with probability $T$, so the outbreak started by a person is the cluster that contains this
person when each edge is kept with probability $T$. We used 1,000 samples for each $T$.

The mean-field threshold is $T_c = \langle k\rangle / (\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle) = 0.12$. Below it,
outbreaks stay tiny. Above it, they grow fast (Fig.~\ref{fig:sir}, left). At $T=0.2$ an average start reaches 4\% of
the faculty, and the best start reaches 20\%. Who is the best place to start? The \textbf{k-core} predicts it best
(Spearman $\rho = 0.93$), better than the degree (0.89), the number of joint papers (0.81) and the betweenness
(0.72). This repeats the result of Kitsak and others: the best spreaders are in the \emph{core}, not simply the people
with the most links. Our top 20 spreaders are all at KGP, in the materials and chemistry core.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sir_spreading.png}
\caption{SIR spreading. Left: outbreak size against $T$. Right: expected outbreak against the k-core of the start.}
\label{fig:sir}
\end{figure}

The faculty network stays inside the two IITs. As a last step, we look at co-authorship with all other Indian
institutes.

\subsection{Partner institutes}
We count the \textbf{shared papers} between each IIT and each Indian partner. KGP works most with \textbf{IISc
Bangalore} (340 papers), IIT Bombay (317) and Jadavpur University (305). Roorkee works most with \textbf{IIT Delhi}
(310), the National Institute of Hydrology (266) and IIT Kharagpur (231) (Fig.~\ref{fig:partners}). These lists make
sense by location and by field. The KGP list has several institutes in Kolkata, for example Jadavpur University. The
National Institute of Hydrology is in Roorkee, and it matches the strength of Roorkee in water research.

\subsection{Similar or complementary research}
Do the IITs work with institutes that do the same research, or with institutes that fill a gap? To measure this, we
take the top 20 research subfields of the IIT and of the partner. Then we calculate the \textbf{Jaccard similarity}.
\[
J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}
\]
The value is 1 if the two lists are the same, and 0 if they have nothing in common.

For the top 25 partners of each IIT, the median is \textbf{0.60}. Thus most partners do \textbf{similar} research. Some
strong partners are \textbf{complementary}. Examples are the Indian Statistical Institute (0.18, KGP), and the National
Institute of Hydrology and the Indian Institute of Remote Sensing (0.21 each, Roorkee). These institutes are
specialised. Thus the collaboration is probably on one topic, such as statistics, hydrology or remote sensing.

\section{Dashboard and Conclusions}
%===========================================================
All these results are also in an interactive website, with a switch for KGP, Roorkee or both. A click on a faculty
member lists all their papers in our dataset, so anyone can check a person's numbers.

To conclude, both IITs almost doubled their science and engineering output in ten years, and about two thirds of their
ranked journal papers are in Q1. Their faculty h-index is about half of a career h-index, because we count only ten
years of papers, and it grows by about one point each year. Environmental Science is high because much water and
climate engineering is filed there. Papers with more than 10 authors are only 2\% of the output, but 6 of KGP's ten
most cited papers. Without them the typical numbers stay the same and only the top moves. Citations are heavy-tailed (lognormal-like rather than a clean power law), and new
citations go preferentially to papers that are already cited. Most citations inside an institute are self-citations,
so we also rank papers without them and correct PageRank for age. Since 2023 Indian research cites the IITs more than
they cite it. Faculty work in tight, hierarchical groups inside each campus. The network is a small world that grew
denser every year. It survives random loss of faculty, but the loss of about 100 bridging faculty would split the two
campuses, and ideas spread best from its core. Each step of this work is a script in
our repository, so anyone can repeat it.
