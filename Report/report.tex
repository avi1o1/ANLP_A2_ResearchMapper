\begin{abstract}
We collected the papers of IIT Kharagpur and IIT Roorkee from 2016 to 2026 and linked them to 1,638 faculty. We study
them at the institution, department and faculty level, and we build a citation network and a co-authorship network.
Both IITs doubled their output, their citations are heavy-tailed, and their faculty work in tight groups inside their
own IIT.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%===========================================================
We look at research at three levels, the \textbf{institution}, its \textbf{departments} and its \textbf{faculty}.
Counts of papers and citations describe each level, but they do not show how the research is \emph{connected}. Networks show this. A \textbf{citation network} shows how
ideas move between papers. A \textbf{co-authorship network} shows how people work together.

This report follows that order. It goes from the institution to the faculty, then to the two networks, and then to
the links with other Indian institutes. Table~\ref{tab:glance} gives the main numbers.

\section{Data and Method}\label{sec:data}
%===========================================================
\textbf{Collecting the papers.} We scraped publication records from public sources. These are Crossref, Europe PMC,
DOAJ, INSPIRE, ORCID, DBLP and Semantic Scholar. We searched in two ways. The first way uses the institute name in
the author affiliations. The second way uses the faculty list of each IIT. This gave 33,393 records for KGP and 25,775
records for Roorkee.

\textbf{Filling in missing values.} Many records had missing fields. We used \textbf{OpenAlex} to get the DOI, type,
venue, ISSN, citations, references, author affiliations and research topic. We used \textbf{Scimago} to get the
journal quartile, by ISSN and publication year. We used \textbf{CORE} to get the conference rank. We did not guess any
value. If we could not find a value, we left it blank.

\textbf{Checking the affiliation.} We kept a paper only if the IIT is in the author affiliations on the paper. This
removes namesakes and papers from before a person joined the IIT. After this check we have \textbf{30,019} papers for
KGP and \textbf{22,844} papers for Roorkee.

\textbf{Linking papers to faculty.} The brief asks for one row for each paper and faculty member. We linked papers to
faculty with five rules, from the most reliable to the least reliable. These are the \textbf{ORCID}, the faculty
profile where we found the paper, the exact \textbf{name}, the \textbf{name and department}, and the OpenAlex author
ID. Extra checks stop wrong matches, for example two faculty with the same common surname. About \textbf{85\%} of the
papers have at least one faculty member (84.7\% for KGP and 84.4\% for Roorkee). We credited the other papers to the
institute.

\textbf{Deliverables.} The four files have 63,122 rows (\texttt{group3\_data}), 544,794 citation edges, 160,214
author pairs and 5,847 Indian partners.

\textbf{Limits.} We include all fields, which are science, engineering, health and social sciences. 2026 is a \textbf{partial year}, and the citation counts are from
October 2026. The h-index uses only the 2016--2026 papers in our dataset. Thus it is lower than a Google Scholar
h-index.

\section{Institution-Level Statistics}\label{sec:inst}
%===========================================================
\subsection{Publication trend}
The simplest measure of research activity is the \textbf{number of papers per year}. We look at it first because it
sets the scale for all other results. More papers give more citations and larger networks. Thus we must keep the size
of each IIT in mind when we compare them.

Both IITs grew steadily (Fig.~\ref{fig:trend}). KGP went from 1,782 papers in 2016 to 3,429 papers in 2025, which is
\textbf{1.9 times} more. Its peak was 3,580 papers in 2024. Roorkee went from 1,335 papers to 2,932 papers, which is
\textbf{2.2 times} more. KGP is larger every year, but Roorkee grows faster. Not all paper types grew at the same rate.
At KGP, conference papers grew 2.4 times and journal papers grew 1.7 times. At Roorkee the values are 2.9 and 2.0.
Preprints went from almost zero to 150 to 200 each year. Thus part of the growth is a change in \emph{how} the IITs
publish, and not only in how much. The drop in 2026 is because the year is not complete.

\subsection{Journal quality}
A paper count does not show quality. The \textbf{Scimago quartile} ranks each journal in its subject by its SJR score.
Then it divides the list into four equal parts. Q1 is the top 25\%. Scimago ranks journals \emph{inside} each subject.
Thus the Q1 share lets us compare quality across fields that cite at very different rates.

About 94\% of the journal papers of both IITs have a quartile. Of these papers, \textbf{64.4\%} at KGP and
\textbf{59.5\%} at Roorkee are in Q1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:quart}). Between 28\% and 30\% are in Q2, and only about 1\% are
in Q4. We expect this result from two of the oldest IITs, which publish mostly in established journals.

For conferences we use the \textbf{CORE} rank. CORE ranks mostly computing conferences. Here KGP is far ahead, with
118 A* papers and 97 A papers. Roorkee has 11 and 37. This result makes sense. Computer Science is the largest
department of KGP by papers (Table~\ref{tab:dept}), and it gives 12.2\% of KGP papers. At Roorkee, Computer Science
gives 8.0\% of papers.

\subsection{Research areas and journals}
Next we ask \emph{what} each IIT works on. For this we use the OpenAlex \textbf{field} of each paper. The research
areas are important for the rest of the report. They control which papers can cite each other and which faculty can
work together. In Section~\ref{sec:india} we use them again to compare the IITs with their partners.

\textbf{Engineering} is the largest field at both IITs. It has 31.6\% of KGP papers and 38.6\% of Roorkee papers. After
Engineering, the two IITs are different. The second field at KGP is \textbf{Computer Science} (12.2\%). The second
field at Roorkee is \textbf{Environmental Science} (11.3\%). This agrees with the large Civil Engineering department
of Roorkee, which is its largest department (Table~\ref{tab:dept}), and with its departments that study water.
Materials Science is third at both IITs, with about 9\%. The top journals of KGP are Physics of Fluids (330 papers),
Scientific Reports (194) and ACS Applied Materials and Interfaces (152). The top journals of Roorkee are Scientific
Reports (133), the International Journal of Biological Macromolecules (132) and Physical Review C (117). All of them
are Q1 journals. Some are broad journals, and others match a strong area of each IIT, such as fluid mechanics at KGP
and nuclear physics at Roorkee.



\subsection{Most-cited papers}
The last measure at this level is the number of \textbf{worldwide citations} of each paper. These papers are the most
visible work of each IIT. But some of them tell us more about the type of paper than about the institute.

Table~\ref{tab:cited} shows the top three papers of each IIT. The top papers of KGP are of three types. These are
\textbf{large collaborations} (the autophagy guidelines, with 4,434 citations), \textbf{software tools} (SymPy and
iFogSim) and \textbf{reviews}. At Roorkee, at least five of the top ten papers are \textbf{reviews or surveys}. Their

paper of Roorkee is also a large collaboration, the European Pulsar Timing Array. This result makes sense. Everyone who
uses a topic or a software tool cites its review or its tool paper. Thus these papers get citations much faster than a
typical research paper.

\section{Faculty and Department Level}\label{sec:fac}
%===========================================================
The institution numbers are sums over people. Now we look at the people, and then at the departments that group them.

\subsection{h-index of faculty}
A faculty member has an \textbf{h-index} of $h$ if $h$ of their papers have at least $h$ citations each. This measure
is useful here because it combines \emph{how much} a person publishes and \emph{how much} other people cite it. One
very large paper, such as the large collaborations above, cannot increase it alone.

The median h-index is \textbf{7} at both IITs. The mean is 9.2 at KGP and 8.7 at Roorkee. The distribution is strongly
right-skewed (Fig.~\ref{fig:h}). Many faculty have a small h-index, and a few have a very large one. The highest value
is 63 at KGP (Narayan Chandra Das, Rubber Technology), and the next is 54 (Makarand M.\ Ghangrekar, Civil Engineering).
At Roorkee the highest value is 42 (Zillur Rahman, Management Studies, and Soumitra Satapathi, Physics). The low median
makes sense. We count only ten years of papers, and recent papers had little time to get citations.

\subsection{Departments}
Departments are between the person and the institute. They are the natural unit to compare research areas inside one
IIT. Table~\ref{tab:dept} shows the five departments with the most papers at each IIT.

At KGP, Computer Science has the most papers, but Civil Engineering has the most citations (42,007). Civil Engineering
includes environmental engineering, and two of the five highest h-index values at KGP are in this department. At
Roorkee, Civil Engineering has the most papers and the most faculty (75). The Q1 share is similar in the engineering
departments, between 50\% and 73\%. Mathematics is much lower at both IITs, at about 44\%. We expect this, because
mathematics journals have lower impact scores in general. Small departments can also be strong. Rubber Technology at

\section{Citation Network}\label{sec:cit}
%===========================================================
Up to now, all citations had the same value. A network lets us ask \emph{who} cites whom.

\subsection{Construction}
Each \textbf{node} is a paper of our two IITs. A \textbf{directed edge} $A \to B$ means that paper $A$ cites paper $B$,
and both papers are in our dataset. The edges have a direction because a citation goes one way, from a newer paper to
an older paper. The network has \textbf{52,584} papers and \textbf{96,030} edges. KGP has 29,997 papers and 53,798
edges, and Roorkee has 22,804 papers and 38,648 edges. The network is very sparse. The density is
$3.5\times10^{-5}$ and the mean in-degree is 1.8. About \textbf{half} of the papers get no citation from another paper
of the two IITs. The largest weakly connected component has 53\% of the papers. We expect this, because the papers
are in many fields that do not cite each other.

\subsection{In-degree distribution}
As we know from our lectures already, the in-degree of a node is the number of edges that point to it. Here it is the
number of papers in our dataset that cite a paper. Its distribution shows if attention is spread evenly or goes to a
small number of papers.

Fig.~\ref{fig:indeg} shows $P(\text{in-degree} \ge k)$ on a log-log scale. The curve decreases slowly over two orders
of magnitude. Thus the distribution is \textbf{heavy-tailed}. Most papers get zero, one or two internal citations, but
a few papers get 40 to 58. This is the ``rich get richer'' effect, or preferential attachment, from our lectures.
People find a cited paper more easily, so they cite it again. The curves of the two IITs are almost the same. This
tells us that the effect comes from how citation works, and not from one institute.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{indegree_ccdf.png}
\caption{In-degree distribution of the citation network (CCDF, log-log).}
\label{fig:indeg}
\end{figure}

\subsection{PageRank}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{PageRank} gives a score to a node. The score depends on how many nodes
point to it \emph{and} on how important those nodes are.
\[
PR(i) = \frac{1-\alpha}{N} + \alpha \sum_{j \to i} \frac{PR(j)}{k_{\text{out}}(j)}, \qquad \alpha = 0.85
\]
Here $N$ is the number of papers and $k_{\text{out}}(j)$ is the number of papers that $j$ cites. The in-degree gives
the same value to every citation. PageRank gives more value to a citation from an influential paper. Thus it finds
papers that are the \emph{base} of other well-cited work.

Our data shows this clearly (Table~\ref{tab:pr}). The top paper by PageRank is a 2016 KGP paper on MoS$_2$
heterojunctions. It has only 20 internal citations and its in-degree rank is 209. But the papers that cite it have many
citations themselves. There is also an \textbf{age bias}. Seven of the top ten papers are from 2016, and the Spearman
correlation between PageRank and publication year is $-0.39$. This makes sense. In a citation network the score moves
almost only from newer papers to older papers. Thus the oldest papers in our window collect the most score.

\subsection{Citation flow with other Indian institutes}
The network above stays inside our two IITs. To see their place in Indian research, we also count citations to and
from other Indian institutes. The \textbf{in-flow} is the number of citations from papers of other Indian institutes to
our papers. The \textbf{out-flow} is the number of citations from our papers to their papers. The \textbf{impact
ratio} is the in-flow divided by the out-flow. A ratio above 1 means that Indian research cites the IIT more than the
IIT cites Indian research.

KGP has an in-flow of 114,443 and an out-flow of 129,758, so its ratio is \textbf{0.88}. Roorkee has 96,963 and
117,255, so its ratio is \textbf{0.83}. At first, it seems that both IITs give more citations than they get.
Fig.~\ref{fig:flow} shows the reason. In 2016 our papers already cited thousands of older Indian papers (the KGP
out-flow was 5,764). But almost no paper had cited them yet (the KGP in-flow was 217), because citations take years to
arrive. The in-flow grows much faster over time. Since \textbf{2023} it is larger than the out-flow at both IITs. Thus
the total ratio below 1 comes mostly from the 2016--2026 window.

The largest Indian source of citations to KGP is IIT Roorkee (3,105 citations). VIT (3,022) and AcSIR (2,885) are next.
For Roorkee the largest sources are VIT (2,565), UPES (2,533) and AcSIR (2,211). Large universities such as VIT and
Saveetha cite our IITs much more than our IITs cite them (the KGP ratio with Saveetha is 4.7).

\section{Co-authorship Network}\label{sec:auth}
%===========================================================
The citation network shows how \emph{ideas} move. The co-authorship network shows how \emph{people} work together.
This is closer to how research groups really work.

\subsection{Construction}
Each \textbf{node} is a faculty member of one of the two IITs. An \textbf{undirected edge} connects two faculty who
wrote at least one paper together. The edge weight is the number of joint papers. The network has \textbf{1,638}
faculty and \textbf{3,329} edges, and its density is 0.0025. On average, a faculty member has 4.1 faculty co-authors,
and 324 faculty have no faculty co-author. The largest component has 1,260 faculty, which is 77\% of all faculty.
Fig.~\ref{fig:net} shows this component. The two IITs make \textbf{two separate clusters}, and only \textbf{90} edges
connect them. This is natural, because the two campuses are far apart and most work happens inside one campus.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{faculty_network.png}
\caption{Largest component of the faculty network. KGP is blue and Roorkee is orange.}
\label{fig:net}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Degree centrality}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{degree centrality} is the number of neighbours of a node, divided by
$n-1$. In this network it is the number of faculty colleagues that a person wrote papers with. Thus it finds the people
who collaborate the most.

The highest values are for Santanu Dhara (KGP, Medical Science and Technology, 52 faculty co-authors), Suman
Chakraborty (KGP, Mechanical Engineering, 49) and Partha Roy (Roorkee, Biosciences and Bioengineering, 44). Medical
science and biosciences are interdisciplinary areas. A project in these areas needs people from many departments, so
we expect a high degree here.

\subsection{Betweenness centrality}
As we know from our lectures already, \textbf{betweenness centrality} measures how often a node is on the shortest path
between two other nodes. A person with a high betweenness is a \textbf{bridge}. Without this person, some groups are
far apart or not connected. This role is different from having many co-authors.

The top bridges are Partha Roy at Roorkee (0.048), and Suman Chakraborty (0.030) and Pabitra Mitra (Computer Science,
0.029) at KGP. Pabitra Mitra shows the difference between the two measures. He has fewer co-authors than the top people
by degree (35), but his co-authors are in many different groups. Many departments use computer science methods. Thus
it makes sense that a computer scientist connects them.

\subsection{Communities}
A \textbf{community} is a group of nodes with many edges inside the group and few edges to other groups. We use the
\textbf{Louvain} algorithm, which finds the groups with the highest \textbf{modularity}.
\[
Q = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i,j}\left(A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\right)\delta(c_i, c_j)
\]
The modularity compares the number of edges inside the groups with the number we expect at random. Here the
communities are the real research groups. These groups can follow department lines, or not.

We get $Q = 0.76$ and \textbf{27 communities} with 5 or more faculty. In 23 of these 27 communities, 90\% or more of
the members are from one IIT. This again shows that collaboration stays inside each campus. The communities are
\textbf{interdisciplinary}. In a typical community, the largest department has only about 44\% of the members. For
example, the largest community has 146 faculty from Roorkee. Biosciences is its largest department, but its members
come from 20 departments. The result is stable. Over 10 random seeds, the mean Adjusted
Rand Index is 0.83.

\subsection{Comparison with random networks}
As we know from our lectures already, the \textbf{clustering coefficient} is the probability that two co-authors of a
person also wrote a paper together. A value such as 0.17 has a meaning only
when we compare it with a baseline. Thus we compare the network with two random models of the same size, with 10
samples each (Table~\ref{tab:null}). The first model is an \textbf{Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi} (ER) graph with the same number
of edges. The second model is a \textbf{degree-preserving} rewiring that keeps the degree of every node.

The real network has about \textbf{90 times} more clustering than the ER graph and 17 times more than the rewired
graph. But its mean shortest path is almost the same as in the ER graph. This is the \textbf{small-world} property.
The network has tight local groups, but the paths between people are still short. This makes sense for research
groups. A new project often brings in a colleague of a colleague. The modularity is also much higher than in the two
random models. Thus the communities above are real, and the algorithm did not create them. The positive assortativity
(0.19) shows that well-connected faculty usually work with other well-connected faculty.

\section{Connections with Other Indian Institutes}\label{sec:india}
%===========================================================
The faculty network stays inside the two IITs. As a last step, we look at co-authorship with all other Indian
institutes.

\subsection{Partner institutes}
We count the \textbf{shared papers} between each IIT and each Indian partner. KGP works most with \textbf{Jadavpur
University} (361 papers), IISc Bangalore (358) and IIT Bombay (342). Roorkee works most with \textbf{IIT Delhi} (333),
the National Institute of Hydrology (267) and IIT Kharagpur (253) (Fig.~\ref{fig:partners}). These lists make sense by
location and by field. The KGP list has several institutes in Kolkata, which are Jadavpur University, IIEST Shibpur and
the University of Calcutta. The National Institute of Hydrology is in Roorkee, and it matches the strength of Roorkee
in water and environment.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{top_partners.png}

\subsection{Similar or complementary research}
Do the IITs work with institutes that do the same research, or with institutes that fill a gap? To measure this, we
take the top 20 research subfields of the IIT and of the partner. Then we calculate the \textbf{Jaccard similarity}.
\[
J(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}
\]
The value is 1 if the two lists are the same, and 0 if they have nothing in common.

For the top 25 partners of each IIT, the median is \textbf{0.60}. Thus most partners do \textbf{similar} research. Some strong partners are
\textbf{complementary}. Examples are the National Institute of Hydrology (0.21, Roorkee) and the Indian Statistical
Institute (0.18, KGP). These institutes are specialised. Thus the collaboration is probably on one topic, such as
hydrology or statistics.
